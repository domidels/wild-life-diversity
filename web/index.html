After the fire: what changed in what people observe?

Satellites record where fire burned. Naturalists record which species they see. Crossing the two, before and after major wildfires, we compare burned land with nearby unburned land to separate the effect of fire from everything else.

01The fire

Burn scar and study zones

H3 hexagons of ~5 km². A cell is burned when satellites detected fire in it; it takes the colour of its most recent fire.

In plain words. The region is cut into small hexagons of about 5 km², roughly 700 football pitches each. Red hexagons are where satellites spotted flames. Blue hexagons are similar land nearby that did not burn: they show what would probably have happened in the red area without the fire. Grey hexagons are set aside: a ring of about two hexagons around the burn scar, where it is unclear whether the land burned, or land hit by another fire. In the figures above, a fire detection is one hot spot seen by a satellite pass, and an observation is one sighting logged by a naturalist.

Example A hexagon where the satellite saw flames on 20 July is red. A pine forest 20 km away that never burned is blue. If birdwatchers report fewer birds in both, the fire is not the cause; if only the red one drops, it may be.

02Who was looking, and when?

All the data here comes from ordinary people who report the wildlife they see, mostly through free apps such as iNaturalist (take a photo of a plant or an animal, the app suggests its name, other users confirm it) or eBird for birds. So before asking what happened to nature, we must ask how much people were looking. Two things change that: these apps gain users every year, so sightings rise everywhere even when nature does not change; and after a fire, burned land is often closed or unappealing for months, so fewer people go there. We measure it in two ways: the number of sightings logged, and the number of different observers who logged them. Each zone is shown as a percentage of its own usual monthly level before the fire (100% = a normal month). If both lines rise or fall together, the cause affected the whole region, not just the burned land.

Burned Control Fire periods

Sightings per month

In plain words. Sightings counts every report logged that month: one photo or one line in a checklist = one sighting, whatever the species and however many individuals. Observers counts the different people (or accounts) behind those reports: someone who posts 50 photos in a month counts once. The two together tell apart “more people came” from “the same few people posted more”. Neither measures how much wildlife there is. Each zone is shown as a percentage of its own usual level before the fire, so a small zone and a large one can be compared; Raw counts shows the actual numbers.

Example Before the fire, a typical month in the burned zone brings 200 sightings from 40 observers. The month after, 50 sightings from 10 observers: both lines read 25%, so a quarter of the usual people came. If instead it were 50 sightings from 40 observers, the same people still came but posted less. If the blue control lines stay near 100% meanwhile, the drop is specific to the burned land: fewer visitors there, not necessarily fewer animals.

Observer names are never stored: GBIF's “recorded by” field is replaced by an anonymous code, used only for counting. A group recording together counts as one observer, and a few sources do not give the observer at all.

Satellite fire detections per month, whole region

In plain words. Each bar is the number of hot spots NASA satellites detected in the region that month. Satellites pass over several times a day and see the ground in squares of about 375 m, so a big fire produces thousands of detections. The tall bars mark the fires; the small ones are mostly farm burning or small blazes.

Example A fire burning for 10 days over 100 km² can be seen in several hundred squares at each pass: that alone gives a bar of a few thousand detections for that month.

03Did the burned area lose species?

The comparison that matters: how the burned area changed relative to the control. Richness is rarefied — counted on samples of equal size — so more observers do not mean more species.

Species richness (rarefied)

In plain words. The more you look, the more species you find, so raw species counts favour the zone with more observers. To compare fairly, we ask: if we picked the same number of sightings at random in each zone and period, how many different species would we get?

Example Zone A has 5,000 sightings and 300 species; zone B has 500 sightings and 120 species. A looks far richer, but 500 sightings drawn at random from A might contain only 130 species: the two zones are in fact close.

Observations

Total over each compared period

In plain words. The total number of sightings in each zone, before and after the fire. It shows how much effort sits behind the species chart on the left.

Example If the burned zone falls from 3,000 to 300 sightings, a tenfold drop in visits, the species count there is based on far less looking, which is why the left chart uses equal-size samples.

In plain words. The verdict does not ask “did the burned area lose species?” but “did it lose more than the nearby unburned land?”. Things that change everywhere (weather, the popularity of nature apps, a lockdown) affect both zones; comparing the two removes them. A negative number means the burned area did worse than expected.

Example The burned zone goes from 100 to 80 species (−20%) and the control from 100 to 90 (−10%). Part of the drop happened everywhere. The fire effect is 0.80 ÷ 0.90 ≈ 0.89, that is about −11%.

04Which species were seen more, or less?

Each bar compares a species' share of observations after vs before the fire, in burned land, minus the same change in the control. Right: relatively more frequent where it burned.

In plain words. For each species, we look at its share of all sightings, before and after the fire, in the burned zone, and compare that change with the change in the unburned zone. Bars to the right: the species became relatively more common in sightings where it burned (×2 = twice as frequent compared with the control). Bars to the left: relatively less common. Only species with enough sightings are shown; hover a bar to see the counts.

Example Before the fire, a woodpecker species makes up 1 sighting in 100 in the burned zone; after, 3 in 100. In the unburned zone it stays at 1 in 100. Its bar reads ×3, to the right: it is seen relatively more where the forest burned, perhaps attracted by insects in dead trees.

New to the region after the fire

Never recorded in the compared period before, at least 3 records after. Often pioneer plants, or simply newly-looked-for species.

In plain words. Species never recorded in the whole region during the “before” period, and recorded at least 3 times after. The number is how many times. This can mean a real newcomer, or simply that someone started looking for it.

Example A small plant never logged in the three years before the fire, then logged 5 times on the ash the following spring, shows up here as “· 5”.

05Did the mix of life change?

Share of observations by group. A move that appears in the burned area only is a candidate fire effect; a move in both zones is a change in what people record.

Too few observations in the burned area to read this chart yet — shown for transparency.

Burned Control Before After

In plain words. For each group of living things, the share it represents among all sightings. The hollow circle is before the fire, the filled one after; the line between them is the change. The top line of each row is the burned zone, the bottom one the control.

Example Birds go from 40% to 25% of sightings in the burned zone but stay at 40% in the control: the fire may have changed what lives, or what gets noticed, there. If birds dropped to 25% in both zones, the cause is more likely a change in observers, for example a plant-identification app becoming popular.

06Did any species' observations move?

Mean latitude of each species' observations, after minus before. Whiskers show ±2 standard errors; filled dots are shifts larger than that. Within one region this often reflects where observers went — a lead to check, not a conclusion.

In plain words. For each species, we take the average north–south position of all its sightings, before and after the fire. The dot shows how far that average moved, in km towards the north (right) or the south (left). The grey line is the margin of error: if it crosses 0 km, the move could be chance. A filled dot means the move is larger than that margin.

Example A lizard was seen on average at one latitude before and 8 km further north after: its dot sits at “N 8”. That does not prove lizards moved; walkers may simply have gone to different places, for instance avoiding the burned south of the region.

07How to read this

Design: BACI

Before-After-Control-Impact. For each metric,
effect = (burned after ÷ before) ÷ (control after ÷ before).
Dividing by the control cancels changes that happened everywhere: more app users, a rainy year, a lockdown.

Example Burned: 1,000 → 400 sightings (×0.4). Control: 1,000 → 800 (×0.8). Effect = 0.4 ÷ 0.8 = 0.5: the burned area got half the sightings expected from the control's trend, a −50% effect.

Limits

  • GBIF records presence, not abundance: these are observations, not populations.
  • Observers avoid burned land for a while, then come back to look — effort itself reacts to fire.
  • Control cells are not random: they may differ in habitat or access.
  • “New to the region” is sensitive to effort and to taxonomic changes.

Pipeline

  1. A monthly Databricks job pulls GBIF occurrences and NASA FIRMS detections into a Unity Catalog volume.
  2. dbt builds bronze → silver → gold tables: H3 grid, burn zones per fire, BACI, rarefaction — tested at every layer.
  3. GitHub Actions exports the gold tables to JSON and publishes this page.
